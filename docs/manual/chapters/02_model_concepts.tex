\chapter{Model concepts and analytical architecture}
\label{chap:model-concepts}

\section{Purpose and modelling philosophy}
PySig is a lane-based traffic-signal analysis package intended for transparent,
reproducible analysis of signalised intersections and, where supported, connected
intersection networks.  The model is deliberately explicit about the distinction
between physical inputs, traffic demand, signal control, calculated capacity and
operational performance.

PySig is pre-release software.  Results should be checked against field observations,
hand calculations and established reference models before they are relied on for
design or investment decisions.  PySig combines published traffic-engineering
relationships with original open-source analytical and simulation algorithms; the
provenance of each important calculation is therefore stated in this manual.

\section{Core object hierarchy}
The fundamental physical hierarchy is:

\begin{verbatim}
Model
  Intersection
    Arm
      Lane
        Movement
    Controller
      Phase sequence
        Phase
          Signal groups
\end{verbatim}

An \emph{arm} describes an approach or departure road at an intersection.  Arms carry
road name, travel direction and an Entry or Exit relation.  A \emph{lane} is the
principal physical and analytical unit.  An Entry lane receives demand approaching the
stop line; an Exit lane receives departing movements.  A \emph{movement} connects an
Entry lane to an Exit lane and carries a movement type, such as through, left or right,
and a signal-group identifier.

The demand hierarchy is separate:

\begin{verbatim}
OD pair
  Route alternative
    Movement sequence
      Lane demand
\end{verbatim}

A route is a sequential path.  Parallel lanes must therefore be represented by
parallel routes rather than by placing two alternative movements consecutively in one
route.

\section{Two calculation engines}
PySig distinguishes the selected traffic assignment from the numerical method used to
calculate lane performance.

\subsection{Simulation engine}
The simulation engine is a deterministic aggregate, fixed-time-step model.  For time
step $\Delta t$ it applies conservation in the form
\begin{equation}
Q_{t+\Delta t}=Q_t+A_t-D_t,
\end{equation}
with finite lane storage, signal permission and downstream receiving constraints.
It is not an individual-vehicle microsimulation.

\subsection{Analytical engine}
The analytical engine represents the queue as a deterministic fluid queue.  Between
events where arrival and service rates are constant,
\begin{equation}
\frac{dQ}{dt}=a-\mu.
\end{equation}
Hence
\begin{equation}
Q(t+\tau)=Q(t)+(a-\mu)\tau,
\end{equation}
subject to $Q\geq0$.  If $\mu>a$ and $Q_0>0$, the exact queue-clearance time is
\begin{equation}
t_c=\frac{Q_0}{\mu-a}.
\end{equation}
The solver splits the analytical interval at this clearance event rather than rounding
the event to a fixed time step.

\section{Conservation}
Traffic should not disappear from the model.  For an OD pair $w$,
\begin{equation}
\sum_{r\in R_w} f_r=q_w,
\end{equation}
where $q_w$ is fixed OD demand and $f_r$ are route flows.  At lane level, admitted
arrivals, departures, residual queue and reported overflow should reconcile.  Where
finite storage prevents entry, the unadmitted quantity must be reported rather than
silently discarded.

\section{Base values and operational values}
Throughout PySig, physical/base quantities should remain distinguishable from
operational quantities affected by interaction.  In particular:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{base saturation flow} describes stop-line discharge under its specified
      saturation-flow method;
\item \textbf{nominal capacity} applies effective green to base saturation flow;
\item \textbf{accessible capacity} additionally reflects shared-lane, priority,
      short-lane or other operational constraints;
\item \textbf{degree of saturation} must identify which capacity definition it uses.
\end{itemize}

This separation is central to model auditability.
